Problem

Too much, added one piece at a time.

A feature problem in appearance, an information-architecture problem underneath — nobody owned the structure, so every addition made local sense and the total kept growing.

Business problem → root cause → band-aid

The match page is the highest-traffic surface in the product — nearly every session starts and ends here. Over years it had absorbed feature after feature — fantasy, points tables, MVP stats, richer commentary filters, polls, media — without ever being re-architected.

Root cause: nobody was doing too much on purpose, and nobody owned the structure. Every addition made sense in isolation; the pressure was only ever to add, never to cut. By the time that became visible, the page had grown to 8–15 tabs with minimal discoverability.

The band-aid: features kept landing as new tabs — the fastest way to ship something without disturbing what already existed. It solved each individual request and quietly compounded the larger problem.

Strategy: reframe this from a feature problem into an IA problem — keep the depth (Cricinfo's real competitive advantage), rebuild the structure holding it.

Strategy

My role, and the reframe that shaped everything after.

The instinct was to cut features to reduce clutter. That wasn't the move — depth was the product's advantage, and users said so too.

Role, team, scope, timeline
  • Role — project lead
  • Team — worked closely with the product manager and the broader product team
  • Scope — structure the IA for a better overall UX on the match page
  • Timeline — roughly six months, shipped in phases rather than as one release: tabs consolidated first, then accordions, then the recent-matches dropdown, then prev/next navigation — each landing separately over the course of the rollout

The harder, more honest question: how do you make the deepest cricket product on the market feel as simple as the simplest one, without removing what makes it deep? That question split into two coordinated efforts, run as one page with one throughline.

Organize
What already existed — accordions, merged tabs, relabeling.
Open up
The page to fans it wasn't reaching — polls, ratings, a lighter layer.
Proving the clutter, not just feeling it

An "accretion timeline" made the drift visible — three core things at launch (live table, scorecard, commentary) had become 8–15 tabs with no single owner tracking the total. Combined with direct user quotes and competitive signal, the clutter stopped being a subjective complaint and became a structural, provable fact.

One page, built for three kinds of fans.

Where We Disagreed

Two calls that didn't go the way the room expected.

Not resolved by seniority or by feel — resolved by putting the belief in front of evidence and letting it hold or break.

The compact layout everyone wanted

Fully designed, evidenced against, never shipped

This wasn't a two-team disagreement — it was a shared belief across editorial, product, and stakeholders: people read at speed and want to compare everything in one glance, so the page should be as compact and above-the-fold as possible. It got fully designed. It was deliberately never shipped.

That belief ran directly against the research — users consistently preferred scanning minimal, digestible information over dense, complete-at-a-glance screens. Shipping compactness on top of an already-dense page would have recreated the exact "information dump" complaint, just more concentrated.

What actually shifted the room was an internal audit — running the compact design past the team itself, not just external users. The same feeling users had originally complained about showed up again, this time from people inside the room: too much data, too compact, hard to read, hard to navigate.

Trade-off, named honestlyKilling this cost real design and engineering time that had already gone into a fully built layout. That sunk cost is exactly what makes not shipping it a harder call than it looks in hindsight.

Points table vs. MVP

Letting usage decide

Points table — kept as its own tab. Usage data settled this one directly: roughly 60–70% of users visited the points table, versus around 20% for MVP. During a tournament, "how many points did my team gain" is urgent, high-frequency information; folding it into a shared tab would have buried the exact thing most people came back for.

MVP — merged into a combined "Stats & Fantasy" tab, against the team's push to keep it standalone with its own "full picture." At roughly a third of points table's traffic, MVP didn't justify the same dedicated real estate.

The rule, stated onceDedicated visibility should scale with how central something is to this specific moment — not with how much space it visually takes up.
Trade-off, named honestlyThe "Stats & Fantasy" label doesn't fully describe MVP — the labeling isn't fully intuitive, and we know it. Usage data can justify a structural decision without making the resulting label good.

What We Shipped

Reveal, don't dump.

Every shipped piece follows the same principle the architecture was built on — including engineering and monetization.

Collapse, then reveal the relevant state

Accordions that open where it matters

Scorecards, match flow, and news moved into accordions, collapsed by default, opening to the currently relevant state — live 2nd innings, not day one.

Trade-off, named honestlyComparing two innings side by side now takes more taps than before, and collapsed content can go undiscovered by someone moving fast. Accepted in exchange for a page that doesn't dump five days of cricket on you at once.

Surface content where it's earned

Media, in context, not tucked away

A wicket in commentary now surfaces its photo/video inline; a ball-by-ball scroller of the last three overs sits in the summary with a shortcut into the full overs tab; a tappable report card links into the full match report.

Getting engineering on board

The architecture reinforces the same principle

Tabs load one at a time, on tap — not all at once on page load. This kept the page light and fast despite the depth it was holding, meaning the engineering itself reinforced "don't load everything at once, reveal it as it's needed." The phased rollout followed the same logic — tabs first, then accordions, then the dropdown, then prev/next — each piece shipped and stabilized before the next landed, rather than launching the full IA change in one risky release.

Making room for monetization without breaking the principle

Two placements, and a line held

The redesign could easily have become an argument for more ad surface — more tabs, more scroll, more inventory. Instead, the team agreed to hold the line: two placements only, one below the scorecard, one sticky, with nothing interrupting the experience in between.

Results

Watching the new paths get used, not just the tab count drop.

People tapped the ball-scroller into the full overs tab. People tapped "full commentary" from the summary into the dedicated tab. People tapped the report card into the full match report — cross-tab snacking, working as designed.

The obvious fix was to show more, faster — and for a while, that's what almost everyone in the room believed, myself included, until the evidence said otherwise. The real fix was building a structure that could hold everything the product needed to say, and having the discipline to know — and defend, with data when needed — which pieces deserved their own space and which didn't.
